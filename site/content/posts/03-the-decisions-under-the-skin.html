System boundary / making the software experiment possible

Blackjack’s architecture combines App Lab model workflows, compatible Qualcomm inference and robotics components, ROS 2 and a physical drive interface. The studies below define the power, cooling and packaging conditions that this stack must operate within. Their role is to enable sustained, observable software behaviour on the robot. Read the deployment architecture and first benchmark protocol.

Start with the intended use.

The ambition is open-ended; each engineering decision needs a defined problem. I translate the intended uses of Blackjack into constraints I can inspect, calculate or test. That gives the work a direction and makes it possible to revise the implementation when the evidence changes.

I bring the same habits to this personal project that I use in engineering and technology leadership: define the objective, identify dependencies, allocate effort where it resolves uncertainty, and make the release decision against the evidence. I use manufacturer data, specialist review, CAD and AI-assisted analysis to extend what I can investigate. I remain responsible for the requirements, trade-offs and accepted revisions.

Sequence the commitments.

Blackjack combines Arduino VENTUNO Q and its Qualcomm Dragonwing IQ8 processor with Waveshare UGV02 hardware, custom metalwork and a developing software stack. Each interface creates a dependency. I separate the commitments so an unresolved supply rating or support detail does not disappear inside a larger order.

The standard components can move towards procurement as their identity, interfaces and availability are checked. Custom metalwork passes a separate review of the Fusion model, flat developments and the workshop’s forming process. The computer and drive share power only after the installed module and combined demand have been characterised. Neural experiments follow a recorded baseline, so a change in behaviour can be attributed to the component being tested.

This sequence is how I allocate effort and control rework: reuse the drive platform, resolve irreversible fabrication choices early, provision later interfaces while the sheet is still editable, and commit the next stage against evidence. When a gate cannot close, I want the record to show the open decision and the work that can still proceed.

A cover has to unfold.

A pleasing enclosure can still be impossible to make with the chosen process. Blackjack’s cover has a specific route: cut one flat sheet, then bend it. No welding. Every cutout belongs to the plane of its panel; every panel needs a valid place in the unfolded blank.

The current BJ-P02 concept v0.32 is a 2 mm cover with nine bends. Its trial blank is 285.2 × 241.1 mm. The separate BJ-P03 concept v0.6 rear plate has two bends and a trial blank of 154.0 × 129.0 mm. The CAD-derived trial DXFs and sheets exist. They still need the shop’s tooling assumptions, bend sequence and release review.

There are practical consequences around each bend: room for the inside radius, relief where panels approach and enough straight material for the tool. The CAD design reserves clearance near neighbouring folds. The bending shop must still confirm minimum legs, tooling access and the forming order. BJ-P01, the board-support plate for the reversible mounting option, is also unfinished.

The fan was only half the answer.

The computer’s cooler moves heat into the air around it. That air still has to leave the bay. Study BJ-STU-002, revision C, compares three configurations of the same enclosure. Its calculations use a mixed-air model and estimated fan curves based on the manufacturer’s catalogue endpoints, rather than a measured installed fan curve.

At 15 W in shade, the original holes-only arrangement predicted roughly 50 K of bay temperature rise. Adding the Noctua NF-A4x10 5V PWM helped, but the original grille left only 356 mm² open above it. Seven slots increased that area to 667 mm². Under the lower estimated fan curve, calculated airflow rose from about 3.0 to 4.2 m³/h.

One switch, two very different loads.

The power requirement sounds simple: one switch for the whole robot. The stock host supply is 5 V, while the Arduino VENTUNO Q needs a different input path. Study BJ-STU-001, revision C, compares switching arrangements and follows the supply through the battery module, output connector, wiring and board input.

The simplest candidate takes a fused branch from the stock switched output P3. That also concentrates both loads in one current path. In the Arduino reference workload used as a peak design case, the board reaches 33 W. Dividing by a nominal 11.1 V gives about 2.97 A for the computer alone, before motor current is added.

The check that can change the design

Waveshare publishes 2 A for the retail UPS Module 3S battery output. The installed module must be identified before applying that reference. A shared feed cannot be accepted simply because the voltage looks plausible.

The barrel-input table lists 12–24 V; the Arduino documentation also discusses operation below 12 V subject to available power. A 3S pack traverses that lower region as it discharges. Board behaviour, voltage drop, connector heating and combined demand therefore belong in the bench test. The fuse rating remains open.

The next measurements have names.

The next mechanical check compares the physical chassis and board with their CAD interfaces. The next electrical check identifies the fitted power module and characterises the computer and drive loads. The next thermal check records the assembled enclosure under repeatable workloads, first in shade, then under documented sun conditions.

Those checks may change parts. That is a useful result for the series: we will be able to show which assumption moved, what it affected and why the revision looks different.

A correction should leave the next decision better informed.

The ventilation work shows the whole cycle. The original perforated cover was assessed against sustained compute. The passive result prompted the addition of a fan. The fan calculation then exposed the restriction above it. Increasing the open area improved the modelled operating point, so the cover changed to v0.32 and the exhaust direction was recorded separately. Hardware verification is the next step.

The earlier options remain useful evidence: they explain why the adopted geometry exists and under which assumptions it was selected. When a test contradicts a calculation, I want the same record to preserve the discrepancy, its cause, the corrective change and the check that demonstrates whether it worked. Future work can then reuse the lesson rather than repeat the investigation.
