It started with a doorway.

Last week, I was watching a small simulated robot try to leave a room. It had a lidar, a doorway to find and a neural network whose weights had been written directly by an AI. No gradient training: the AI wrote the coefficients, examined recorded runs and revised them.

The first interesting outcome was a failure. From the middle of the room, the robot kept circling. I suggested heading towards a wall. The AI worked out how to express that behaviour in the weights, and a later revision found the wall, followed it and crossed the doorway.

That experiment, on dum-e in ROS 2 and Gazebo, stayed with me. During a run, the language model was absent. The small network was the controller, with ordinary code handling sensor reduction and command safeguards. Watching a few coefficients turn into movement made me want to try something more ambitious.

The question behind Blackjack

What can a neural network do when it has a body and a hardware platform that lets it think and act efficiently in the real world?

The doorway experiment was a spark. It made me want to explore neural intelligence with access to a physical world: sensors to observe it, compute to interpret what is happening, and a body through which decisions have consequences. I want to build a platform with enough room to follow that curiosity as it grows.

Seeing and driving give us somewhere to begin. From there, I want to ask broader questions. Can the robot connect what it sees with what I ask it to do? Can it retain useful context from an earlier interaction? Can it turn an instruction into a sequence of actions, recognise when the result differs from its expectation and adjust? Which of these behaviours can run usefully on board, within the power and timing constraints of a mobile machine?

Those questions will lead to different models and experiments. The tiny network from dum-e is the origin of the project, rather than a template every future experiment must follow. A perception model, a language or vision-language model, and a control policy may each have a role. Their value will show in what the assembled robot can actually do.

ROS 2 gives those experiments a practical foundation: sensor interfaces, communication, motion and a way to inspect what happened. Some neural components may work alongside that infrastructure; others may take over a particular responsibility. I want the experiments to tell me where each approach is useful. The broader aim is to discover what this kind of intelligence becomes capable of when the hardware lets it operate in the world.

Choosing a software ecosystem: Arduino VENTUNO Q and Qualcomm.

I chose Arduino VENTUNO Q because the platform brings an accessible model and deployment ecosystem together with compute and a real-time microcontroller. Arduino App Lab, Qualcomm AI Hub and Edge Impulse offer starting points for local language, vision and speech applications. That lets me concentrate the experiment on what happens when useful models share a robot, rather than spend the whole project assembling an unrelated toolchain.

Arduino’s VENTUNO Q model catalogue includes Qwen and Gemma families, vision-language models, Whisper speech recognition and object detection. My first run will record the exact model package, quantisation, runtime, execution backend and input data on the delivered board.

The Qualcomm Dragonwing IQ8 (QCS8275) provides heterogeneous CPU, GPU and NPU compute; the STM32H5F5 offers a separate real-time domain. I want to exploit that division deliberately: perception and language on supported inference paths, ROS 2 for orchestration and observability, bounded commands to the drive, and microcontroller experiments with explicit timing responsibilities. The software architecture explains the intended stack and its compatibility gates.

The software research has already produced a useful finding: the current QRB inference package and QIR SDK release documentation describe evaluation-kit targets, which must be distinguished from the VENTUNO Q board and its BSP. I know that compatibility boundary is there. A dedicated session will map and test the supported deployment route before I rely on those components. The stack guide records the finding and the investigation.

The question is how much useful behaviour the complete system can sustain. A model’s standalone throughput is one input. Camera-to-result latency, power at the board input, thermal throttling, command timing and success on a defined task determine whether it belongs on a moving robot.

My first plan was to put the Arduino VENTUNO Q inside dum-e. After trying the available arrangements, I could not find an installation I was happy with. The computer needs space around it for connectors, cables and cooling. Making the board fit on paper would not make the robot serviceable.

I looked for a dedicated kit instead. Among the platforms I found, none gave me the combination of technical fit and visual character I wanted. So the project changed shape: design a rover around the board, using an engineering process I could document from the first decision.

Waveshare UGV02: keep the wheels, design the integration.

Before drawing a chassis, I checked whether I needed to. A drivetrain is already a substantial project: motors, transmission, wheel geometry, encoders, a controller and battery packaging. Rebuilding all of that would pull attention away from the question that started this.

I chose the Waveshare UGV02 as the locomotion platform. Its existing drive electronics, deck and manufacturer CAD gave me an interface to work against. The selection followed checks of the available space, mounting geometry, equipment and power arrangement. Reusing a documented base lets me spend the project’s effort on compute integration, the enclosure and neural behaviour. The stock supply path still needs a bench decision. Waveshare’s UGV02 documentation defines the starting platform; it does not establish compatibility with this new host by itself.

The stock motor controller stays for bring-up. The target software connects ROS 2 velocity commands to its serial interface, with encoder and IMU feedback coming back into the stack. The Arduino VENTUNO Q becomes the host. An enclosure gives the assembly its own silhouette while preserving access to the connections we will actually use.

An open question. A disciplined way to pursue it.

I start with what I want the platform to make possible. The implementation follows from requirements, alternatives and evidence. A robot intended for sustained onboard inference raises different power and cooling questions from a board briefly running a demonstration. Outdoor use adds solar load. Future positioning and connectivity affect the space reserved in the very first enclosure.

My work as an engineer and technology executive shapes how I approach this. I set the direction and acceptance criteria, use specialist expertise and the tools available to me, review the evidence and own the decisions. Manufacturer documentation, CAD, calculations, AI-assisted analysis and design review each contribute to the work. The record needs to make their contributions and limits understandable.

Requirements have identifiers; source files and measurements have references; decisions and revisions preserve the reasoning. If a test contradicts an assumption, I want to know which choice depended on it, correct the affected design and retain the lesson for the next iteration. An error becomes useful when its cause and correction are recoverable.

The project adopts references including ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 for requirements and ISO 10007 as configuration-management guidance, alongside mechanical, electrical and software references appropriate to each deliverable. The engineering note explains their application and connects the expected use scenarios to the studies.

The first software evidence has a protocol.

Once the board arrives, I will start on the bench with the documented YOLOX-Small App Lab route, then establish a ROS 2 inference path through the compatible Qualcomm runtime and packages. For the same recorded camera input I will report sustained inference frequency in Hz, image-to-result latency, board input power in W, bay or inlet air temperature and SoM temperature in °C. The model and software revisions travel with the result. There are no on-device Blackjack measurements in this design episode.

Next I will introduce the robotics workload, drive activity and enclosure as separate changes. That makes a slowdown or thermal limit diagnosable. A language or vision-language model then gets a bounded task, with completion criteria, intervention counts and failures retained. The component and software guide sets out the initial measurement sequence.

The shape has a job.

I could not find a ready-made cover compatible with the Arduino VENTUNO Q integration I wanted. I designed the entire Blackjack cover myself in Autodesk Fusion, working from the Waveshare UGV02 geometry and the interfaces of the equipment it has to house.

I wanted the rover to have a recognisable profile: a low black cover, inclined sides, an open rear recess and the number 21 cut into the flanks. Then the less photogenic questions started determining the details. Can a connector be inserted? Can the cable bend? Can the warm air leave? Can the cover be made from a flat sheet?

The cover mounts to the UGV02’s existing accessory mounting rails. Its fasteners engage hammer-head T-nuts retained inside the rail slots. Those rails already provide an interface for installing equipment; I use that interface to attach the enclosure I designed around the computer.

Assembly development / provisional bench target

0.8 N·m per M3 cover screw is my starting target for joint validation, with steel screws of verified property class 8.8 or higher and steel T-nuts. It is not a released assembly torque. The nut reacts inside the rail; the torque driver is applied to the screw head.

A generic steel M3 class 8.8 reference gives 1.15 N·m under specified friction assumptions. I have deliberately selected a lower trial target because the rail lips, nut engagement and sheet bearing also govern this joint. A screw-only table cannot qualify the assembly. The actual fastener grade, nut supplier, rail capacity, engagement and tightening condition must be confirmed before applying this target; any lower supplier limit takes precedence.

For vibration resistance I propose LOCTITE 222, a removable low-strength threadlocker, on the metal threads, subject to the joint review. Its effect on tightening friction and its curing behaviour belong in that review; the reference friction assumptions cannot simply be transferred to treated threads. After qualification, use the agreed tightening procedure, allow the specified cure, add witness marks and inspect after the first recorded vibration run. Witness marks reveal movement; they do not lock the joint.

References: Beckhoff steel-fastener torque table and its friction assumptions; Henkel LOCTITE 222 technical data. The 0.8 N·m trial target is my engineering choice, not a torque specified by either manufacturer for this rail assembly.

I will commission a sheet-metal workshop to laser-cut the flat blank and form the bends from my design. The cover, BJ-P02, is developed as a single 2 mm sheet with nine bends. There are no welded panels. Its trial flat blank measures 285.2 × 241.1 mm, derived from the CAD model. The rear antenna plate, BJ-P03, has its own flat pattern. The CAD model and trial developments define the intended part. The workshop must still review its tooling, bend sequence and fabrication drawings before manufacture.

That grille change is a useful example of what I want the series to show. The board already has a cooler, but the cooler still needs fresh air. I compared the original perforated enclosure without a bay fan, then with a fan, then with more open area above that same fan. The calculation changed the metalwork before any metal was cut.

In the conservative 25 W direct-sun case, changing the fan grille from holes to slots raised the modelled outside-temperature threshold from 22 °C to 33 °C. Those are estimates for the electronics bay under the study’s assumptions. The assembled rover will have to earn its own operating limits. The design notebook shows the comparison.

Make room for the next question.

The first functional configuration is deliberately small: drive, front depth camera and the Arduino VENTUNO Q. The enclosure also makes space for positioning and connectivity from the beginning. GNSS RTK and LTE are commissioned later, but their antenna provisions belong in the first mechanical build.

A roof mount provides alternatives: front depth camera with a lidar, or the pan-tilt assembly with its camera. The reveal shows a possible accessory configuration. The turret camera specification and cable allocation remain checks to close before that configuration becomes hardware.

The BOM now brings together the base, computer, sensors, Click boards, antennas, cooling, cabling and fasteners. It also identifies the unresolved items. A fuse without a selected rating and a board support without a finished drawing are engineering work to finish, even when the rendering already looks complete.

This week: a design. Next week: orders.

This first episode closes the presentation of the project: the studies, 3D assembly, renders, design film, trial laser-cut and bend documents, accessory selection and draft BOM. It gives Blackjack a form and a set of decisions we can now test.

The next episode moves into procurement. I will check availability, finish the order-critical details, review the fabrication package and place the orders. Fabrication release follows the design review and the bending shop’s feedback. Delivery dates will set the physical build schedule.

I am planning a weekly series with something worth watching in every episode: a sheet becoming a shell, the first power-on, a camera’s view of the room, the first movement, then experiments in perception, interaction and autonomous behaviour. The initial build gives us the means to explore. What we discover will help decide the next question. The code belongs behind those moments, where its consequences are visible.
